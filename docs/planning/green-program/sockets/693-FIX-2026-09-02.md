# #693 fix — a timed-out poll is decided against the peer's publication instant, and the kernel says which

Branch `fix/693-poll-wake-loss`, off `main` @ `3d601400`. Companion to
`693-RCA-2026-09-02.md`, which established what is repaired here.

## 1. What was wrong

#693 records a blocking `poll()` on a connected TCP fd returning `ready=0`,
`revents=0x0000` after its full 5000 ms timeout while a peer process wrote and
exited, and states its undecided fork verbatim:

> **Not** established: whether the peer's `send()` was actually issued and its
> readiness lost, or whether the peer's own `recv` of the token never completed
> so it never wrote at all.
> <!-- claim-lint:ok: verbatim quote of #693's own text; the RCA settled it at
>      368/368 peer branches and 13/13 orderings, 693-RCA-2026-09-02.md §4-§5. -->

The RCA answered it: on 13 of 13 reproductions, and on the specimen #693 itself
preserves, the peer's write landed AFTER the poll's deadline. The poll's zero
was the correct answer for a socket nothing had yet been written to. Two
defects made that undiagnosable:

1. **The verdict decided from inputs that could not separate the two cases.**
   `stage_late` reported `late_lost_wake` — a kernel wake-loss claim — from
   `ready != 1 || revents & POLLIN == 0` alone
   (`userspace/programs/src/poll_tcp_oracle.rs:441` on `85596f62`). The peer's
   publication instant was not among its inputs, and on that arm it was not
   even read: the arm reaped the peer and reported without touching the socket.
2. **The kernel was silent.** A `sys_poll` that times out emitted no record of
   whether readiness had been published inside its own window, and the
   connection kept no such record either — `rx_buffer` holds what arrived, not
   when. The one fact that decides the question lived in the kernel and went
   unstated, which is why the investigation had to reconstruct it from syscall
   ordering across 13 of 13 preserved reproductions.

## 2. The repair

### 2.1 Userspace: the timeout arm recovers the peer's stamps before deciding

`userspace/programs/src/poll_tcp_oracle.rs`. On a timeout return the parent now:
emits the probe (unchanged), reaps the peer and reads its exit code, and then
collects the peer's payload — starting from whatever the probe's *destructive*
non-blocking read already took off the socket. That last point is load-bearing:
on a genuine lost wake the probe's read is the one that succeeds, and the bytes
it takes are the payload whose stamps decide the verdict. The pre-fix probe
discarded them.

With `write_ms` in hand:

| observation | verdict |
|---|---|
| peer exit code is not `sent` | `late_peer_no_publish` FAIL — #693's second candidate, now nameable |
| `write_ms < deadline` AND the probe found bytes on the socket | `lost_suspected`: the SHAPE of a lost wake, REPORTED and retried against a wider delay, not a FAIL from this side (§2.6) |
| `write_ms >= deadline` | `published_after_deadline`: the publication did not enter the window |
| `write_ms < deadline`, probe found an empty socket | `not_readable_at_return`: the send had not reached the socket when the poll gave up |
| `published_after_deadline` or `not_readable_at_return`, Race mode | retry against a doubled poll ceiling (5 s → 10 s → 20 s); on exhaustion `late_peer_overrun` FAIL carrying the measured overrun |
| `published_after_deadline` or `not_readable_at_return`, Forced mode | the arm passed: the classification is correct |
| `lost_suspected`, either mode | retry against a doubled peer delay, at most twice; on exhaustion emit `LOST_SUSPECTED_UNRESOLVED` and complete the trial (§2.6) |

`deadline` is `entry + timeout_ms`, computed from the parent's stamp taken
immediately before the syscall. The kernel starts its countdown at or after
that, so this is a LOWER bound on the kernel's deadline, which is the direction
that keeps the verdict sound.

Raising the ceiling rather than the delay is what makes the retry effective, and
it is free when the trial then succeeds: the poll returns when the peer
publishes, not when the timeout expires.

A `[POLL_TCP_ORACLE:LATE_PUBLISH:…]` line is emitted on each timeout arm,
passing or failing, carrying `anchor`, `entry`, `deadline`, `returned`,
`write_ms` and `late_by_ms`. That is the quantity the RCA had to reconstruct.

Why the verdict needs BOTH facts is §2.4: each stamp on its own lies in a
direction that manufactures #693's error again, and the batteries caught both.

### 2.2 Userspace: a stage 4 that forces the classification on each boot

The classification arm was reached by accident, at whatever rate the peer
happened to overrun by more than five seconds. Stage 4 (`forced`) makes it
happen on purpose: a 150 ms poll against a 500 ms peer delay, so the
publication lands after the deadline by construction. A verdict that cannot
tell "published after my deadline" from "you lost my publication" now fails
deterministically instead of once in twenty-four boots — see mutation 693-U in
§4.

**Caveat, round-2 review F11.** This is a tendency the code does not
enforce, after the §2.6 demotion: `LostSuspected` does not check
`trial.mode`, so a Forced-mode trial that happens to land in that arm can exit
through `LOST_SUSPECTED_UNRESOLVED` with `late_by_ms: 0` -- a stage-4 PASS
with no classification at all, `forced_late_by_ms=0` the only tell (visible in
the 693-U mutation serial, §4). Not reached on an unmutated boot: `write_ms >=
deadline` is what decides `Forced` mode's outcome, and `FORCED_TIMEOUT_MS =
150` against `FORCED_WRITE_DELAY_MS = 500` (§2.2) puts the peer's write on the
far side of that deadline by construction; §5's bullet on the
`lost_suspected` ladder already gives the count this predicts -- 50 of the 50
aarch64 boots in §3.1 decided `published_after_deadline` on the forced arm and
the 50 x86 boots in §3.2/§3.3 read `ready_lost=0` and PASS, 0 of the 100
landing in `LostSuspected` on stage 4. Reached only by 693-U's mutation, which
forces the verdict past that arithmetic. No gate asserts
`forced_late_by_ms > 0`.

### 2.3 Userspace: the peer is anchored to the parent's own pre-send stamp (#694)

The `entry > token_ms` precondition compared the parent's poll entry to the
instant the PEER was scheduled to read the token. `delay_ms` controls how long
the peer waits AFTER that instant, so widening it did not move the peer's write
relative to the parent's poll entry: the five doublings were five flips of one
coin, and 4 of 24 beast KVM boots lost all five and emitted `late_window_missed`
(#694). The token now carries `anchor`, a stamp the parent takes before it
sends; the peer sleeps to `anchor + delay_ms` and echoes the anchor back. The
precondition becomes `entry <= write_ms`, and each doubling now strictly widens
the room the parent has to get from its `send()` to its `poll()`. The echo check
refuses a payload left over from an earlier attempt.

This lands with #693's repair because #693's repair adds a retry that re-rolls
this precondition: without the anchor the extra trials would have made the
ladder exhaust MORE often on x86 than it does on main.

### 2.4 The two facts, and why one is not enough

The x86 KVM batteries reddened twice on this branch's own bytes, and both reds
were the fix's predicate committing #693's error again one instant to the left.
Both are repaired; both are why the verdict now needs two facts rather than one.

**Neither serial is preserved, and that is a gap in this document.** The two
boots below ran on intermediate heads during the iteration and their run
directories were not committed before they were reclaimed, so 0 of the 2
specimens that shaped this design can be re-read at the landed bytes. The 12
`.txt` files in `serials/693-fix/` are 3 aarch64 gate boot serials, 1 gate
output, 3 mutation boot serials, 3 mutation driver outputs and 2 x86 driver
outputs; 0 of the 12 is one of the two boots below. Against this arc's own rule -- preserve failing
serials -- that is the wrong way round, and the 8 stamps quoted below should be
read as quoted measurements rather than as ones a reader can check here. What a reader CAN check is the consequence: the two-fact predicate is in
the code, and mutation 693-K exercises the half that the second specimen
motivated (§4).

**Boot 20 of the first 25-boot KVM battery — `returned` is not the deadline.**
A 150 ms stage-4 poll entered at 39696 and returned at 40389: 693 ms elapsed,
because the parent did not get the CPU back for 543 ms after its budget expired
at 39846. The peer published at 40093 — 247 ms AFTER the deadline and 296 ms
BEFORE the parent's `returned` stamp. Scored against `returned` that reads as a
lost wake; the poll had in fact already decided, correctly, on an empty buffer,
and the kernel's own check (which compares against `deadline_ns`) stayed silent
on that boot. The comparison moved to `entry + timeout_ms`.

**Boot 14 of the second 25-boot KVM battery — `write_ms` is not the
publication.** A stage-3 peer stamped `write_ms=38488`, 762 ms before its
39250 deadline, and the poll returned 0. But `write_ms` is stamped BEFORE the
peer's `send()`, so it is a lower bound on the publication: this peer was
descheduled between the stamp and the syscall. The kernel read `rx_len=0
publish=none_in_window` at its deadline, and the parent's probe, taken the
instant the poll gave up, agreed: `rescan_ready=0 … nbread_err=EAGAIN`. The
verdict now also requires the probe to have found bytes — kernel state read
directly rather than a stamp that can lie. Under mutation 693-K the same probe
reads `rescan_ready=1 rescan_revents=0x0001 nbread_n=23`, so the two cases
separate on the fact rather than on a threshold, and the RCA named that fact in
advance.

The authoritative detector is the kernel-side marker in §2.5, and after this
round it is the ONLY one that fails a gate on a lost wake. Two userspace arms
could previously call a correct kernel defective on their own, and both are now
demotions that report and retry instead of failing: `lost_suspected` (renamed
from `late_lost_wake`) and `woken_by_clock_suspected` (renamed from
`late_woken_by_clock`, round-2 review finding F1) -- both described in §2.6.
The userspace verdict corroborates the kernel's and does not fail a gate on its
own. On 5 of 5 boots
under mutation 693-K the kernel marker fired and the userspace probe agreed
with it.

### 2.5 Kernel: the publication instant, and a marker at the source

* `kernel/src/net/tcp.rs` — `rx_buffer` becomes private and gains
  `publish_rx()`, the one path by which received bytes enter it and the one
  place `rx_publish_ns` is stamped. The 6 append sites are converted; a new
  append site outside the module does not compile. An empty payload is not a
  publication and does not move the stamp.
* `kernel/src/ipc/poll.rs` — `tcp_rx_publication()` reads `(rx_publish_ns,
  rx_len)` for a connected TCP fd.
* `kernel/src/syscall/handlers.rs` — `sys_poll` keeps `entry_ns` and, on a
  timeout return, calls `poll_report_timeout`, which emits one of two lines per
  polled TCP fd:
  * `[POLL_TCP_READY_LOST]` — bytes were published into this connection
    strictly inside the poll's own window, are STILL buffered, and the poll is
    returning without POLLIN. The last in-loop scan runs at the deadline and
    reads the buffer live through the same connection lock, so it had to have
    seen them: this is a contradiction in kernel state. Gate-failing.
    The "still buffered" clause is what keeps it sound (a publication drained by
    another thread is not a loss), and the strict `< deadline_ns` is the other
    half (bytes landing after the deadline are an on-time timeout).
  * `[POLL_TCP_TIMEOUT]` — the ordinary case, for polls of at least 120 ms,
    carrying the publication's offset from entry or `publish=none_in_window`.

Both use `serial_println!`, not `log::`: on aarch64 the `log::` sink is a second
UART and the aarch64 gates boot QEMU with a single `-serial file:`, so a
`log::error!` marker would be invisible to the gates meant to fail on it.

### 2.6 Which side is allowed to say "lost wake"

The kernel side, and only the kernel side. Round 2 of the review found that the
userspace `late_lost_wake` FAIL could still fire on a correct kernel, because
neither of its two inputs is read at the instant the poll decides:

* `write_ms` is stamped BEFORE the peer's `send()`. A peer descheduled in
  between publishes later than its stamp says — measured at 762 ms on beast KVM
  (§2.4).
* `readable_at_return` is the probe's read, taken after the syscall returned AND
  after the parent was scheduled again — measured at 543 ms after the budget
  expired on beast KVM (§2.4).

A peer whose `send()` lands between the kernel's last in-loop scan (which runs
at or after `deadline_ns`) and the parent's probe satisfies both halves on a
poll that answered correctly for the state it read, and the kernel's own
detector correctly stays silent on that boot — so the two detectors would
disagree and the gate would go red on the userspace half. That is the exact
mis-attribution class #693 was filed about, one instant to the left.

Ruling R93 settles it: `[POLL_TCP_READY_LOST]` is the gate-failing authority for
a lost wake, and the userspace arm reports and retries. So:

* the verdict is renamed `lost_suspected` and emits
  `[POLL_TCP_ORACLE:LOST_SUSPECTED:…]` carrying 13 of the 13 fields the FAIL carried, plus the attempt number;
* the retry WIDENS the peer's delay, which is the doubling that discriminates:
  a stamp that merely landed near the deadline is pushed past it and the next
  trial decides `published_after_deadline`, while a kernel that really drops
  publications reproduces the shape at 80, 160 and 320 ms alike (693-K
  publishes 80 ms into a 5 s poll);
* `LATE_MAX_LOST_SUSPECTED_ATTEMPTS = 2`, and exhausting it emits
  `[POLL_TCP_ORACLE:LOST_SUSPECTED_UNRESOLVED:…]` and COMPLETES the trial. This
  arm does not fail the gate on its own.

Detection power is unchanged, because the detector that fires under 693-K is the
kernel one: it fired on 5 of 5 boots for the fix slot and 3 of 3 for the round-1
reviewer, on the same boots the probe read `rescan_ready=1 … nbread_n=23`.
<!-- claim-lint:ok: 5 of 5 and 3 of 3 mutation-693-K boots, section 4 below and
     the round-1 review's own reproduction table -->
What the demotion gives up is the userspace FAIL on those same boots; what it
buys is that a correct kernel cannot be called defective by this arm. The guard
against the kernel authority going quiet is the gates' anti-vacuity requirement
that `[POLL_TCP_TIMEOUT]` be PRESENT (§2.7), not the userspace verdict.

**A second arm was demoted the same way (round-2 review finding F1).**
`late_woken_by_clock` fired a gate-failing FAIL whenever a poll that returned
`POLLIN` still measured `elapsed >= timeout_ms` between the parent's own entry
and return stamps -- its own comment called the shape "the lost wake wearing a
passing revents." The kernel-side detector cannot reach that boot at all:
`[POLL_TCP_READY_LOST]` is gated on `ready_count == 0`
(`kernel/src/syscall/handlers.rs:4165`), and this arm only runs when the poll
returned POLLIN, so a boot that hits it is one the kernel had no verdict on.
<!-- claim-lint:ok: read at kernel/src/syscall/handlers.rs:4165 on this
     branch, round-2 review F1 -->
§5 has the preserved specimen and the rates. It is demoted the same way as
`lost_suspected`:

* renamed `woken_by_clock_suspected`, emitting
  `[POLL_TCP_ORACLE:WOKEN_BY_CLOCK_SUSPECTED:…]`;
* the retry widens the poll's own timeout ceiling, which is free for the same
  reason it is free in `late_peer_overrun`: the poll returns when the peer's
  bytes are ready, not when the timeout expires, so a larger ceiling only buys
  the parent more room before the clock could end things;
* `LATE_MAX_WOKEN_BY_CLOCK_ATTEMPTS = 2`, and exhausting it emits
  `[POLL_TCP_ORACLE:WOKEN_BY_CLOCK_UNRESOLVED:…]` and COMPLETES the trial. This
  arm does not fail the gate on its own either.

### 2.7 Gate wiring

`[POLL_TCP_READY_LOST]` is pinned as a failure in 7 gate scripts
(`run-aarch64-service-sequence-gate.sh`, `-boot-test-strict.sh`,
`-boot-test-native.sh`, `-prod-profile-boot-test.sh`, `-full-test.sh`,
`-arma609-arm.sh`, `-refusal-drain-gate.sh`) and in
`scripts/x86-gate-verdict.sh`. Four of those additionally require
`[POLL_TCP_TIMEOUT]` to be present: the absence of a marker a healthy tree does
not emit for months is not by itself evidence that its reporting path is alive,
and the ordinary line from the same function is what turns "no lost-wake marker"
into a reading. `scripts/x86-gate-verdict.sh` gets the failure check only —
whether an x86 profile contains a blocking TCP poll depends on #697.

## 5. What this does not establish

* It does not close #737, the kernel page fault at `0x8` in TCP teardown. No
  shared mechanism was looked for beyond noting that the signatures do not
  overlap.
* The userspace side no longer names a lost wake at all (§2.6). A boot on which
  the kernel loses a publication AND its reporting path is broken would be
  green: the oracle would report `lost_suspected` twice, emit
  `LOST_SUSPECTED_UNRESOLVED`, and pass. The guard against that is the gates'
  requirement that `[POLL_TCP_TIMEOUT]` be PRESENT, which is a marker a healthy
  boot emits twice, and it is a weaker guard than an independent verdict would
  be. It is the trade R93 chose over a userspace FAIL that can fire on a correct
  kernel, and `LOST_SUSPECTED_UNRESOLVED` is a grep-able bucket if a later round
  wants to pin it.
* The `lost_suspected` retry ladder and `LOST_SUSPECTED_UNRESOLVED` are
  unexercised outside mutation 693-K. No unmutated boot on this branch has
  produced the shape: 50 of the 50 aarch64 boots in §3.1 decided
  `published_after_deadline` on the forced arm, the 50 x86 boots in §3.2 and
  §3.3 read `ready_lost=0` and PASS, and the 3 unmutated boots the round-2
  review ran were 3 PASS with `ready_lost=0`.

       review's reproduction table -->
* **`woken_by_clock_suspected` (round-2 review finding F1) is likewise
  unexercised by any battery in §3, and it fired -- as the pre-demotion
